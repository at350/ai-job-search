# Writing Style Guide

This is the source of truth for how the user writes across all application materials: résumés, cover letters, fellowship and application short-answers, personal essays, and networking notes. It covers two registers (see Two Registers below) and their personal voice.

**Authoritative reference for résumé and cover letter formatting:** your school or career center's own résumé guide, if you have one. Save it under `documents/references/` and name it here so this skill can cite it. The rules below apply alongside that guide; where the two disagree, say so in the review rather than silently picking one.

## Two Registers

Match the register to the document, they are not the same:

- **Résumé bullets:** no personal pronouns, fragments, action-verb-first. See the Bullet Point Style section.
- **Everything written in first person** (cover letters, application questions, fellowship essays, the "about me" type prompts, outreach): the user's personal voice. See Personal Voice below. This is real first person, with "I", contractions, and personality. Do not write these in stiff résumé-bullet style.

## Critical Rules

1. **NO em-dashes (--).**  Use commas, periods, or restructure the sentence instead.
2. **NO cliches or filler phrases.** Cut: "I am passionate about", "I believe I would be a great fit", "leverage my skills", "hit the ground running", "drive results", "synergies".
3. **NO generic buzzwords** without concrete backing. Every claim must be supported by a specific example or fact.
4. **NO apologetic or overly humble language.** Not "I think I could contribute" but "I bring X, demonstrated by Y."
5. **NO unverified company claims.** Every company-specific statement in a cover letter (partnerships, product names, technology descriptions, expansions) must be independently verified via WebFetch or WebSearch before inclusion. Do not trust reviewer agent research at face value. If a claim cannot be verified, rephrase it in general terms or omit it.
6. **Reframe emphasis, not substance.** Some framing of experience toward the target role is expected. But apply the **interview backtrack test**: could the candidate comfortably explain this bullet in an interview without backtracking? If they'd have to say "well, what I actually meant was..." then it's too far. Specifically:
   - **OK:** Reordering experience to lead with what's most relevant; using natural synonyms for the target domain; emphasizing one aspect of a broad role.
   - **Flag it:** Combining academic + industry experience into a single claim that implies it was all industry; describing work using the posting's specific terminology when the actual work was adjacent but not the same.
   - **Never:** Claiming experience the candidate doesn't have; implying they worked in a domain they haven't.
   When a bullet falls in the "flag it" zone, present it to the user after drafting with: "This bullet is a stretch because X. Keep, soften, or drop?" If the evaluation experience match score is below 50, warn before proceeding to drafting that extensive reframing would be needed.
7. **NO "I liked the line in the posting" or any close variant.** it is AI-generated template filler, and it reads that way whichever posting it is pointed at. Refer directly to the company or the role when a specific observation actually matters, and omit the observation when it does not earn its place.

## Tone
- **Warm but direct.** Friendly and approachable, but confident without arrogance.
- **Conversational professional.** Not stiff corporate-speak, not casual chat. Think: how a confident person talks in a good job interview.
- **First person, active voice.** "I built" not "a system was developed by the candidate."
- **Demonstrate, don't state.** Instead of "I am a team player", write a specific example of teamwork and its outcome.

## Personal Voice (cover letters, application questions, essays)

This section is the model for any first-person application writing. It starts empty: fill it from the user's own real writing, then let the learning loop keep it current.

**How to populate it.** Collect the strongest first-person things the user has already written (fellowship essays, club and internship applications, cover letters, cold emails) into `documents/private/`. Read them before drafting anything first-person, and record here what actually recurs: their openings, their sentence rhythm, the concepts they return to, the words they never use. Quote real lines as calibration samples, never to paste into a new application.

### Register map

People write in more than one first-person voice, and the register is set by the individual recipient, not by the company's brand voice or the document type alone. Name the target register before writing any first-person answer longer than about three sentences, and say which one you picked in the review.

Start from these and rename them to match what the user's samples actually show:

- **Polished essay** (fellowships, "why you", reflective prompts): narrative, one strong conceptual hook, an argument built across full paragraphs.
- **Professional / structured** (consulting, finance, pre-professional clubs): organized point by point, values-forward, judgment and mentorship language.
- **Casual / energetic** (hackathons, tech clubs, startup applications): warm, fast, contractions, direct enthusiasm. Do not over-formalize a fun prompt.
- **Short post-interview thank-you email:** roughly 80-100 words, one real callback to something said on the call, one or two hard numbers at most, close on next steps. Never a compact cover letter, never a resume recap.
- **Founder cold email and the founder-read application box:** the user's raw wording preserved, uneven sentence lengths allowed, lead with the useful thing, make a direct ask.
- **Peer-to-peer note before an in-person event:** student-to-student looseness, no credentials in the note, a real question rather than a low-friction hello.
- **Follow-up message after meeting someone at a booth or event:** short, points at earlier proof instead of restating it.

For any outreach register, retrieve the exact approved sample from `documents/private/approved-outreach.md` before drafting. A summary of a voice is not the voice.

### Short free-text boxes: the credential-free answer

On a box of roughly 250 characters, the resume already carries the evidence, so employers and metrics inside the box read as filler. Write a position the user holds, one observation quoted from something they have actually watched or read (pull it from `documents/library/`, do not invent one), and a plain statement of what they want.

Offer a **bracketed-gap frame** on the first pass of any short first-person box: draft the shape and leave bracketed slots for the user to fill with their own observation or affiliation detail. Handing back a fourth full draft is usually worse than handing back a frame they can finish in their own hands.

### Signature structural moves

These carry most first-person answers. Reach for them, and add the user's own as they emerge.

1. **Story, then lesson, then forward application.** A concrete scene, the principle drawn from it, then how it gets used next.
2. **Anchor motivation in a real personal stake.** Ground "why I care" in true experience, never abstract passion. Never invent the stake.
3. **Scale the impact: local, then national, then global.** A good skeleton for any "how will you make an impact" prompt.
4. **Reframe the question to show thinking.** Flip the obvious framing ("how do we increase adoption" becomes "why do users distrust the product"). Demonstrate insight rather than asserting it.
5. **Prove with specifics and named methods.** Concrete figures and named tools beat adjectives every time, as long as they are true.

### Openings

- A conceptual hook for serious essays, or drop straight into a vivid, real scene.
- Warm and direct for casual applications.
- **Avoid the generic opener** "I am writing to apply for the [role]."

### Sentences and rhetoric

- **Ground abstractions in one sensory detail.** One concrete image carries more than a paragraph of theory.
- **Anchor an essay on a single crisp concept** and return to it.
- **Confidence backed by proof.** State wins plainly and always pair them with evidence.
- **Antithesis sparingly.** "Not X, but Y" is a rare deliberate beat, never the default cadence; the Critical Rules ban its common forms.

### Closings

Forward-looking, tying the story back to where the user is heading. End on direction, not a thank-you cliché.

### Recurring themes and signature stories

Keep a short list here of the honest throughlines and the stories the user reuses, plus an **overuse flag** list of phrases that have already appeared near-verbatim in more than one application. Rotate them so nothing reads canned.

### Mechanics

- **No em-dashes.** Use commas, periods, parentheses, or restructure.
- Numerals for figures, name the specific tool or method, respect the word limit and the one-page cap.

### What to avoid

Boilerplate cover letters with a generic opener, filler praise, and no specific hook or stake. When a draft starts sounding like that, add a real hook and a real personal stake, then run the `humanizer` skill to strip anything AI-sounding.

## Application Headline (Best Practice)

The subject line / headline of the application should be engaging and specific, not generic.

**Bad:** "Application for Sales Engineer Position" / "Ansogning til stilling som ingeniør"
**Good:** "[Your specialty] specializing in [relevant keyword from posting]"

Formula: **[Title/education] + [relevant keyword from the job posting]**

## Scannable Structure (Best Practice)

Employers scan applications quickly. Structure for easy reading:
- Use descriptive subheadings that reflect content (not just "Introduction" / "Body")
- Include industry-specific keywords in headings where natural
- Write concisely - eliminate filler language
- One page maximum (hard rule)

## Forward-Looking Framing (Best Practice)

The cover letter is **not a CV repetition**. It should be forward-looking:
- Focus on **tasks you can solve for the employer**, not just what you've done before
- Describe your approach: methods, tools, knowledge you'll bring
- Explain what positive outcomes the employer can expect from hiring you
- Use 1-2 brief past examples only to back up forward-looking claims

## Cover Letter Structure

**Superseded.** The structure below is upstream boilerplate. The binding cover-letter spec is CLAUDE.md (3-5 paragraphs, business-letter format, salutation, opening naming the exact role, evidence paragraphs, a closing that thanks them and asks to discuss, then "Sincerely, <Your Name>" with no contact block; paragraphs, not bullet lists, by default) and `06-cover-letter-templates.md`. Where anything below differs from those two, they win.

### Opening Paragraph
- State the role and why you're writing (1 sentence)
- Immediately connect your background to the role (1-2 sentences)
- Make it specific to this company/role, not a template opener

### Body Paragraphs - Task-Solving Focus
- Lead with the most relevant experience for this specific role
- Frame content around **which of their tasks you can solve and how**
- Describe your approach: methods, tools, and knowledge you'll bring
- Default to paragraphs; a bullet list is a rare exception, per `06-cover-letter-templates.md`
- Include at least one example that shows initiative
- Include 1-2 brief examples of past success, but keep the focus forward-looking

### Motivation / Why This Company (place early)
- The **first section** after the opening should explain why you're applying to *this specific company*
- Use language and themes from the job posting and company website
- Focus on how you'll contribute to their goals, not what you gain from employment
- If you spoke with someone at the company, reference the conversation naturally

### Company-Specific Paragraph
- Show you've researched the company (mention specific projects, values, or market position)
- Explain why this company specifically, not just "a company like yours"
- Connect domain knowledge to their business context

### Closing
- Brief, confident, forward-looking
- Thank them for their consideration and ask to discuss: "Thank you for considering my application. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how I can contribute to the team."
- No begging or over-enthusiasm

## Bullet Point Style (résumé)

The standard bullet formula: **ACTION VERB + TASK + PURPOSE/RESULT** (skill + what + why/impact).

- Start with an action verb from a career-guide verb list (Built, Designed, Analyzed, Synthesized, Led, Managed, Streamlined, etc.)
- **No personal pronouns** in CV bullets (no "I", "my", "our")
- **No full sentences**, fragment form
- Be specific: numerals (6 not "six"; 30% not "thirty percent"), name the tools, quantify the outcome
- Aim for 2-5 bullets per experience
- Vary the action verb (don't start every bullet with "Built" or "Designed")

## Language for Different Role Types

### Technical/ML roles
- Lead with programming languages, ML frameworks, specific model architectures
- Mention datasets, data volumes, pipeline complexity
- Include independent projects

### Domain-specific roles
- Lead with domain expertise and specific methods
- Frame technical skills as tools that enhance domain analysis

### Consulting/Advisory roles
- Lead with stakeholder communication, project coordination, client interaction
- Emphasize ability to bridge technical and business perspectives

### Leadership/Senior roles
- Lead with project management, mentoring, course development
- Frame advanced degrees as evidence of independent project delivery

## Multi-language Applications
- Default to the language of the job posting
- Cover letters in the posting's language should feel natural, not translated
- Slightly warmer, more personal tone may be acceptable in some languages

## Patterns Observed in Past Applications

Empty until the user has applications to learn from. As approved cover letters and answers accumulate, record the reusable patterns here: the opening shape, the pivot into the most differentiated experience, the paragraph that pairs a technical claim with operational scale, the closing shape. Also keep a list of **bullets and phrases that have appeared verbatim more than once**, so letters to companies in the same ecosystem do not read template-y.

**AI tooling references.** When mentioning agentic coding or AI tooling, name the specific agent that actually fits the project and the employer rather than a generic phrase like "AI coding agents." Default to the tool the user genuinely used most for that work. **Never name a competitor's tool on an application to that competitor**; pick the neutral or matching option instead.

## Learned from Approvals (auto-updated by the learning loop)

Append one dated entry per approved document or answer, newest at the bottom, headed `### YYYY-MM-DD, <company> <artifact> - <THE LESSON IN CAPS>`. Each entry states what was rejected, what the user said in their own words, what shipped, and the reusable rule. When a later entry contradicts an earlier one, the later entry wins and should say so.

Two standing rules for this log:

- **Quote the user's own correction verbatim.** A paraphrase of "less resume yap, more me" loses the instruction.
- **Dictated replacement copy is the authority.** When the user rewrites an AI-sounding answer themselves, preserve their wording, cadence, hedges, and repeated words instead of re-polishing them.
