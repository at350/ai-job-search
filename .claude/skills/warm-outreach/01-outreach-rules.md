# Networking and Outreach Rules

**Tooling:** Do NOT use Apollo for the personal job search. Apollo is reserved for work purposes only. For finding and researching connections, use LinkedIn (via browser), web search, and warm-intro paths instead.

**Before writing any outreach message,** check `documents/playbook/index.md` for `outreach-cold`, `outreach-warm`, and `referrals` claims that fit the target (see Playbook Retrieval above). The baseline rules below still apply.

**Also scan `documents/private/stated-beliefs.md` for a hook.** The outreach rules below demand a specific opener, and the beliefs file is where the specific openers actually live: every position the user holds, with provenance and source. Match an entry's topic against the target's domain, and lead with the idea, not the fact that they watched a video.

- **Every entry is in play, including Raw.** Verbatim, Approved, and Paraphrase entries can be drafted from directly. A **Raw** entry that fits the target is surfaced to the user with a request to develop it on the spot, not skipped; once they expand it, draft from what they said. Never write their opinion for them, and treat media-library items with no take on file the same way: the value is the user's opinion, not a creator's summary.

- **When an entry cites a media-library file, quote third parties only from that file's "Exact quotes" section**, with attribution to the original speaker. Everything else is paraphrase and must be written as paraphrase.

- **Never repeat a number a library file's fact-check table flagged as unverified, imprecise, or wrong.** A cold email to a founder is exactly where a wrong statistic is most expensive, and several saved items are vendor marketing whose headline numbers did not survive checking. Where the beliefs entry names a banned number, that ban travels with the belief.

- **The playbook still governs how the message is written.** The beliefs file supplies the hook; the playbook supplies the structure, the ask, and the close. Where they conflict, the playbook wins, and CLAUDE.md wins over both.

- A beliefs entry is one option for an opener, not a requirement. A firsthand problem the user hit in the target's product still beats a secondhand idea, and for the short-list startup targets the built-proposal tactic below outranks both.

**What makes an outreach message bad (avoid):**

- Generic opener with no specific hook

- Leading straight with "can you give me a job/referral"

- Vague claims about skills instead of concrete proof

**What makes an outreach message good (do this):**

- Open with something specific: a post they wrote, something they worked on, a problem the user has seen firsthand

- **Choose the opener by who is reading, and prefer something they made over something they hold.** A practitioner, founder, or engineer is opened with something they produced: a guide they wrote, a company they started, a club they ran, a product decision they shipped. A job title, an employer, or a school is not a hook, because it cost them nothing to acquire and every other student opens with it. A company recruiter is opened differently, with the exact role and, where one genuinely exists, the name of the internal contact who pointed the user there, because a recruiter is sorting requisitions rather than evaluating an admirer. **Never name a referral who did not refer.** Where a target has produced nothing findable, fall back to a firsthand problem in their product.

- **Read the target's own writing for register, not only for a hook.** Researching a person for something specific to say is only half of it. Read a few of their posts and match their sentence length and formality: a founder who writes short and direct gets a short and direct message, and someone who writes long analytical posts can take more. This applies only where the target has enough public writing to read a register off, which covers founders and active operators; where they do not, stay conventional. It never reaches cover letters or application free-text boxes, where the employer sets the register and the salutation stays.

- **A founder who reads their own inbox is an audience where a funny or strange opener is allowed.** With that specific reader, being odd enough to be read beats being correct enough to be ignored, because the real competition is deletion. **The scope is exactly one reader type** and does not widen: not recruiters, not university recruiting, not a cover letter, not an application free-text box, not anyone inside a large-company pipeline. In all of those the employer sets the register (see the rule above on register being set by the employer) and nothing changes. **Three limits inside the scope.** The opener is the only part that gets to be strange; the substance underneath still follows the ordinary rules, still names something specific they found, and still ends on one clear ask. It never invents a shared history or a fact, however obviously it is meant as a joke. And it never displaces a built proposal where one is warranted, because a built proposal is what actually earns the reply.

- **Founder inbound at seed and YC-stage startups: first message under 240 characters, exactly three beats.** Who the user is, what they built that is relevant to them, and why they believe in what they are building. The longer cold-email format stays for recruiters and larger companies. AI-assisted drafting stays allowed on one condition: the final text must read as the user's own, not as generic AI output, and it must respect the length cap. Founder-directed drafts get an extra pass for template smell before sending.

- Show what the user actually brings, with evidence (a project, a result, something they built)

- **For a small number of high-priority startup targets, build something for them instead of describing past work.** A short written proposal specific to that company (a concrete problem the user noticed in their product plus a specific fix) outperforms a link to past projects, because it proves the work rather than claiming it. Scope: **YC companies, a16z-backed or similar early-stage portfolio companies, and Series A through B startups**, where a founder or senior operator reads their own inbox. Wasted on large-company recruiting pipelines, where cold email does not reach a decision maker. Two conditions: the analysis has to actually be good, since a proposal that misreads their constraints reads as arrogant, and this costs hours per target so it is a short-list tactic, never the default outreach motion.

- Short ask with a clear time commitment ("20 min call" beats "coffee sometime")

- **Give them an easy out that still routes.** Close with a line like "if this doesn't fall under your team, I'd appreciate any direction on who might be best to connect with." It asks for a pointer rather than a referral, so a no still returns a name, and it never uses the word "referral," which is what makes it easy to answer.

- **When someone asks the user whether they know anyone for a role, the answer names the user.** A request for a referral is a hiring need stated out loud by the person who owns it, which is better information than a posting and warmer than a cold message. Answer it by putting their own name forward first, then supplying other names if they have them. The same move converts the reply the easy-out line above is designed to produce: when a contact says the role is not theirs and names who owns it, ask for the introduction in the same message rather than closing the thread. This applies only where they can actually do the job, since the value comes from a real need being named.

- If the user is missing a skill they want, show they are actively closing the gap

- **Keep it short enough to read in one screen.** Say the hook, the evidence, and the ask, then stop. Three independent sources now say the same thing, and a founder reading their own inbox says AI has made inbound applications longer and worse, so a message that is correct and complete but long now reads as machine output regardless of quality. This is a ceiling on length, not permission to cut the evidence: on short-list startup targets the built-proposal rule still applies, and the fix there is a tight message carrying the proposal, not a long message.

- **Consider the blank connect where an application is already live.** One school of thought, worth testing against your own results: at a company where the application is already submitted, a noted invitation spends the single message on information the reader can already see, and an unaccepted invitation is a note nobody reads. Sending the invitation blank and holding the hook for the follow-up after acceptance is a legitimate default for recruiters, alumni, engineers, and executives alike. **Research the hook anyway and file it**, because the post-acceptance message is where it gets used. This never applies where there is no live application: a founder, a cold alum at a company not applied to, or anyone whose first message is the only contact still needs the note, because no packet stands behind the invitation. Record which variant the user prefers once they have stated it.

**Contact triage: The user's binding constraint is calendar time, not message volume.** They work multiple concurrent roles and cannot coffee-chat everyone, so every outreach run must sort contacts into three tiers and say which tier each person is in. Sending is cheap; scheduling is what they run out of. The goal of outreach is a named human attached to their application, which at most companies is a two-minute referral form, not a relationship built over calls.

- **Tier 1, live call (cap at roughly 1-2 per week).** Reserve synchronous time for people who can actually move a decision: the hiring manager, someone on the specific team, or the recruiter who owns the req the user is in. Also anyone at a company where they have already passed an assessment or interview, since the application is live and a conversation can move it. Only Tier 1 gets a "20 min call" ask by default, and only Tier 1 permits holding a slot on the recipient's calendar.

- **Tier 2, asynchronous first.** Alumni at large companies they applied to cold, anyone whose title does not touch their req, and any speculative contact. Default to a short message with a specific question or a direct referral ask, answerable at their keyboard, because replying to an email is far easier for a stranger than giving up 30 minutes. **The user may attach a booking link and name a concrete window when they have something worth a call, but the message must stand on its own without one, so a keyboard reply is always a complete answer.** Do not open a scheduling thread, and do not hold a slot on a Tier 2 contact's calendar; a held slot is a Tier 1 move. The cap on live calls is unchanged, so a Tier 2 call that gets booked comes out of the same 1 to 2 weekly slots.

- **Tier 3, skip.** Large centralized campus pipelines where no reachable person touches the decision. Do not spend a message.

Two habits that follow from this:

- **Batch the sending.** Draft outreach in blocks (roughly two 30-minute sessions a week, 8-10 messages out), rather than opening a scheduling thread per person.

- **Ask directly instead of routing through a relationship-building step.** For Tier 2, "I applied to X and completed the assessment. Would you be open to referring me, or pointing me to whoever owns that req?" is shorter, honest, and costs neither party a calendar slot. Pair it with the easy-out line above so a no still returns a name. This is a deliberate exception to the standard career center advice against asking for a referral in a first message, which assumes the sender has unlimited time to build the relationship first; that advice still applies to Tier 1.
  - **This rule assumes a live application, and that precondition is now explicit.** Every clause of the model message presupposes a requisition to refer into. With nothing submitted, the same words ask a stranger to vouch for the user for an unspecified role, which costs the recipient more and returns less, because a referral with no req attached usually cannot be acted on.
  - **So where the user wants a named human at a company they have not applied to, submit the application first, then send the direct ask.** Applying takes minutes and it makes the approved message available immediately. This changes the order of operations rather than the message.
  - **Where they cannot apply yet** (no open req, a stealth company, or a specific team they are targeting), send one cheap factual question that converts into an application instead, such as whether that team is hiring interns this cycle or whether the req routes through university recruiting. Answerable at their keyboard, and not a favour.
  - What stays banned either way is the relationship-building thread with no ask in it, which is the failure this rule exists to prevent.

- **Startup application artifacts must be real tools, not portfolio pages that explain an idea.** The artifact must accept real external input, perform useful executable work, produce inspectable output, and remain useful after the application is sent. It needs tests and a runnable install or execution path. A frontend is allowed only when it is a working client for the underlying tool. A static one-page lab, simulated dashboard, or concept page does not qualify.

- **Put the creativity in the action, proof, or delivery.** A clever opener attached to a generic application is not creative outreach. The memorable element should give the recipient something real to inspect, try, use, or respond to, such as a verified issue, useful tool, thoughtful physical delivery, or company-specific demonstration.

- **Agree on the value before implementation.** Before building anything for startup outreach, state in one sentence who will use it, what real input it accepts, what useful output or action it produces, why the target company should care, and how the recipient can try it. If those answers are weak, do not build yet.

- **Explore first, then commit once.** When the user asks for broad ideation, generate and rank the requested idea space before implementing. Rank on company relevance, evidence strength, recipient friction, durability, and time to a credible result. Get agreement on the direction and delivery format before a substantial build or deployment.

- **Stop the scrap cycle.** After two rejected directions, stop generating polished variants. Restate the user's objections in their words, define the acceptance test, and choose the smallest proof that meets it. Do not keep adding live data, dashboards, animation, or technical surface area when the recipient value is still unclear.

- **Make the first click self-explanatory.** A founder should understand the observation or tool within seconds. Public links should open without sign-in when appropriate. Numbers need interpretation and a decision they support; a screen full of metrics is not value by itself.

- **Batch the drafting.** Prepare outreach in blocks (roughly two 30-minute sessions a week, 8-10 messages) rather than opening a scheduling thread per person. Sending still requires the user's approval of the exact recipient, channel, and message.
